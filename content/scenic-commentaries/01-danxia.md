## English Commentary

### Opening

Good morning, everyone. Welcome to Guangdong. Today we visit **Danxia Mountain**, about **forty-five kilometers** northeast of Shaoguan City — a world-famous red-stone landscape and the namesake of a whole geological term. In Lingnan travel talk, it ranks among the **four most famous mountains** of the region: **Danxia, Luofu, Xiqiao, and Dinghu**.

### Stop A: How the red land was born — and how it got its scientific name

Geological studies show that **twenty-five million years ago**, this place was a vast, low-lying lake. Later, movements of the earth’s crust lifted the land; the lake water receded; and the sediment at the bottom gradually **oxidized into red rocks**. That is how the glowing red cliffs you see today were formed.

In the **1930s**, Professor **Chen Guoda** of Sun Yat-sen University made an intensive study of Danxia Mountain and other red-rock mountains in South China. He named this kind of red-rock land configuration the **“Danxia Landform.”** The name was soon accepted worldwide. From then on, wherever you find landscapes of red sandstone conglomerate with sheer, red cliffs, geologists call them Danxia landform.

Places featuring Danxia landform can be found on **all continents except Antarctica** — for example, the Grand Canyon in the United States and some areas in Saxony, Germany. But **Danxia Mountain itself** is the **largest**, the **most typical**, and the **most beautiful** of its kind.

### Stop B: China Ruby Park — the big picture in numbers

Please look around. The mountain covers **319 square kilometers**, with **over 600** red rock peaks standing out against green woods — like a garden of ruby sculptures. That is why it is also called **China Ruby Park**, or China Red Stone Park. In **1995**, the Chinese State Council approved it as a **geological nature reserve**.

Now, let’s walk the classic viewing route — from river to bridge, from “yang” to “yin,” and finally into the temple.

### Stop 1: Jinjiang River — “The Elephants Crossing the River”

Please look at the rocks beyond the **Jinjiang River**. They look like a herd of elephants wading across toward us — trunks, tusks, ears, and eyes all lifelike. So this sight is named **“The Elephants Crossing the River.”**

### Stop 2: Yangyuan Bridge — “The Sleeping Belle”

Now we stand on **Yangyuan Bridge**. Please look to your **right**, at the hill in the distance. It looks like a young girl lying down to sleep: from right to left, her head, neck, chest, and belly are clearly outlined. This sight is called **“The Sleeping Belle,”** or **“The Beautiful Girl Blocking the River”** — *Yu Nü Lan Jiang*.

### Stop 3: Yangyuan Hill and Yangyuan Stone

Next is **Yangyuan Hill**, meaning “the Hill of Male Sex.” The famous stone column here — **Yangyuan Stone** — is about **28 meters** high and **7 meters** in diameter, and it looks strikingly like the male organ. Geological studies show it was once part of the cliff; over **three hundred thousand years**, Nature severed it from the mountain and carved it into this shape. In the old days, men were regarded as superior to women, and people prayed for more sons to carry on the family name, so pilgrims often worshiped this stone.

### Stop 4: Yinyuan Cliff — Nature’s Adam and Eve

Nearby is **Yinyuan Cliff**, formed some **one hundred thousand years ago**. Just as God sculptured Adam and Eve in the Garden of Eden, Nature carved a stone husband and wife here in Danxia. What a wonderful world this is!

### Stop 5: Biechuan Temple and the chameleon dragon

Finally, **Biechuan Temple**, built in **1662**, the first year of Emperor Kangxi’s reign. In the Qing Dynasty it was thronged with pilgrims and ranked among the **three largest Buddhist monasteries** in northern Guangdong.

Inside the **Sakyamuni Hall**, please look at a rock shaped like a dragon. It is called **“Bian Se Long”** — the chameleon — because its color changes with the seasons: light green in spring, dark green in summer, yellowish green in autumn, and brownish yellow in winter.

### Closing

So Danxia is both a **geology classroom** and a **picture book**: an ancient lake turned red, a name that traveled the world, elephants in the river, a sleeping girl on the bridge, a stone couple of yin and yang, and a temple dragon that changes color. If examiners ask about formation, naming, or any of these scenes, you already have the answers in this route.

Thank you. I’m ready for your questions.
