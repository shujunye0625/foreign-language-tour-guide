## English Commentary

### Opening

Hello, everyone. Welcome to the **King’s Tomb Exhibition Area** of the **Nanyue King Museum**. Two thousand years under Elephant Hill still speak through jade, bronze, and gold. Before we meet the tomb owner, one short map of history: in Pre-Qin times this region was home to the **Nan Yue** people; after Qin unified Lingnan, **Nanhai Prefecture** was set up here — and today’s Guangzhou grew from that seat of government. At the start of the Western Han, the former Qin general **Zhao Tuo** founded the **Nanyue Kingdom**, promoting Han–Yue fusion. The kingdom lasted through **five kings** for **ninety-three years**, until it submitted to the Han in **111 BC**.

### Stop A: Who is buried here?

The tomb owner was **Zhao Mo**. He styled himself **“Emperor Wen”** and was the **second king** of the Nanyue Kingdom. He came to the throne in the fourth year of Emperor Wu’s Jianyuan era and reigned **sixteen years**, from **137 to 122 BC**.

### Stop B: Discovery and the tomb’s shape

The tomb of Zhao Mo was discovered in **1983**, **20 meters** under **Elephant Hill** at **Jiefangbei Road** in Guangzhou. It covers about **100 square meters**, is built of **750** blocks of red sandstone, and is divided into front and rear sections with **seven chambers** in all.

This is the most important Han tomb so far found in South China — the **largest** in size, the occupant of the **highest** political and social status, and the **richest** in unearthed relics. It is considered one of the **five major archaeological finds** in modern China.

### Stop C: Palace ruins nearby

Archaeologists also trial-excavated about **500 square meters** of Nanyue palace ruins at the original site of Guangzhou’s **Children’s Park** (on the east side, as the Chinese text notes). The spot under excavation matched the position of **Chang Le Gong** in the Han palace at Chang’an — so was this Nanyue’s “Chang Le Gong”? Further research is still needed.

### Stop D: Fifteen people buried alive

In the tomb, **fifteen persons** were found buried alive with the dead: one in the **front chamber** — a **“Jing Xiang Ling,”** perhaps a eunuch; one in the **outer coffin**, probably a **cart-driver**.

Burying the living with dead sovereigns had prevailed in Central China in the Shang and Zhou dynasties (**1766–770 BC**), but was largely abolished in the Han Dynasty (**206 BC–AD 220**). Finding so many sacrificial persons here shows this cruel custom was **still practiced** by Nanyue’s ruling class.

### Stop E: Seals — gold imperial seal and nine jade seals

Please keep two seal stories clear — they are different objects.

First, the **gold seal of Emperor Wen**: the only extant emperor’s seal from the Qin and Han dynasties unearthed by archaeology. Records say imperial seals were white jade with a tiger-shaped handle; but Zhao Mo’s seal is **gold with a dragon handle**, cast in Nanyue, and used in his lifetime.

Second, the **jade seals**. Of the **nine** seals unearthed from this tomb, **three** were found on Zhao Mo’s body in the main coffin chamber, inscribed respectively **“Zhao Mo,” “Tai Zi” (the prince), and “Seal of Emperor” (帝印)**. Together they prove that Zhao Mo **overstepped his authority** to style himself emperor, breaking away from the Han to exercise local power.

### Stop F: The silk jade garment and the giant jade disc

Jade garments were a burial practice peculiar to the Han Dynasty. Elsewhere they were usually sewn with gold, silver, or bronze strands. Zhao Mo’s garment was sewn with **silk strands** — the **first and only** silk-thread jade garment found in China so far. This jade garment is **1.73 meters** long, made of **2,291** jade pieces strung with **red silk strands**, forming bright, beautiful patterns.

Of **56** jade discs (*bi*), **47** were in the main coffin chamber; one is **33.4 cm** in diameter — the **largest** of its kind unearthed in China.

### Stop G: Bronze — dagger-axe, tiger tally, and chime

Among the weapons, one **copper dagger-axe** bears the inscription **“王四年相邦张义.”** It was made under the supervision of **Zhang Yi** in the reign of King Hui of Qin and later brought to the South.

A **bronze tiger tally** with gold inlay is the **only** gold-inlaying tiger tally still existing in China — a credential for troop movement or diplomacy.

Especially worth mentioning is a chime of bronzes called **“Gou Diao”**: **eight** pieces, cast with the epigraph **“Made in the Official Conservatory in the Ninth Year of Emperor Wen,”** showing they were made in Nanyue in **129 BC**. After more than **2,100 years**, they can still produce clear, accurate notes.

### Stop H: A Persian silver box — and why Guangzhou cares

One silver box, by shape and ornament so different from traditional Chinese silverware, and by chemical analysis of the pills inside, is believed to be a product from overseas **Persia**, with contents likely a kind of **Arabian medicine** — evidence of early maritime contact.

Many bronzes and other artifacts show excellent workmanship with distinctive local features. They reflect Nanyue metal-casting skill and serve as important evidence for the **founding history of Guangzhou**.

### Closing

Hold this chain: **Zhao Tuo’s kingdom → Zhao Mo the second king → 1983 Elephant Hill → fifteen sacrificial persons → gold dragon-handle seal + nine jade seals (three on the body) → silk jade garment → bronze treasures → Guangzhou’s origins**. That is the full white-paper toolkit for this exhibition area.

Thank you. I’m happy to take your questions.
