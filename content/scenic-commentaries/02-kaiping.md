## English Commentary

### Opening

Hello, everyone. Today we explore **Kaiping Diaolou and Villages** in Jiangmen — an open-air museum where overseas Chinese dreams meet fortress walls. In the map of Lingnan architecture, these towers stand as **world cultural heritage** fortresses, alongside other Guangdong folk landmarks such as Xiguan houses, arcade buildings, Hakka round houses, and ritual compounds like the Chen Family Temple.

### Stop A: What are they — honors and numbers

A Kaiping *diaolou* is a watchtower-like building that combines **defense**, **residence**, and **Chinese–Western architectural art**. It has been hailed as **“a Typical Work of Overseas Chinese Culture”** and **“the World Architectural Art Museum.”** It is a **key cultural relic under state protection**. At its peak there were more than **3,300** towers; today about **1,833** remain registered; and **twenty** of the finest are on the **UNESCO World Heritage List**.

### Stop B: Why build houses like fortresses?

You may wonder why returned overseas Chinese built such towers. First, public order was bad; banditry was a headache, and better-off returned families became robbery targets. Second, Kaiping is **low-lying land**, and **floods** were frequent. So these solid high-rises served both as **defense against bandits** and as **refuge from floods**.

### Stop C: Three functional types

Functionally there are three forms:

1. **Communal towers** — jointly built by several families as temporary refuge;  
2. **Residential towers** — built by rich individual families as fortified homes;  
3. **Watchtowers** — mainly for guarding against bandits.

Remember: **communal, residential, watch**.

### Stop 1: Zili Village — Mingshilou, the finest

In **Zili Village**, **Mingshilou** is the best of all. Built in **1925**, it is a **five-storey** reinforced-concrete **residential tower**. On top sits a Chinese–Western **hexagonal observation pavilion**; on the fifth floor, each of the four corners has a blockhouse — also called a **“swallow’s nest.”** The tower is huge and imposing, with heavy iron doors and strong iron windows, luxuriously decorated and fully furnished for daily life.

### Stop 2: Ruishilou and Fangshi Denglou

**Ruishilou** is the **highest and most luxurious** tower in Kaiping: **nine storeys**, about **25 meters** high, armored concrete, with elegant traditional Chinese interiors — rated the **No. 1 tower** in Kaiping.

**Fangshi Denglou**, built in **1920** by the Fang family, is the most typical **watchtower**: open land, spacious views, and a full defense kit — **electric generator, searchlights, and guns**.

### Stop 3: Li Garden — name, layout, and East meets West

Now we come to **Li Garden** (*Li Yuan*), in **Beiyi Village, Tangkou Town**. Built in **1936**, it is a villa garden mixing Chinese and Western styles. On the tall arched gate are the characters **“立园.”**

Why “Li”? The American Chinese owner **Xie Weili** took the name from his own name, and it expresses the idiom **Xiu Shen Li Ben** — “cultivating one’s moral character is the foundation of success” — written on an archway in the garden. The same idea appears in many inscriptions and couplets.

The garden has **three areas**: villa area, big garden area, and small garden area. Main sights include the **“Li Yuan” arched gate**, the **“Xiu Shen Li Ben” decorated archway**, **four bridge pavilions**, and two Roman-style structures — the **“Bird’s Nest”** and the **“Vine Pavilion.”**

In the villa area, residential buildings combine Chinese and Western elements: foreign-style main structures, some with Chinese palace-like roofs; inside you find Western fireplaces and pendant lamps, **Italian ceramic tiles**, Chinese wooden furniture (including rosewood-style pieces), folk-story murals, and **gilded wood-carvings**.

### Closing

Kaiping is safety, wealth, and cultural mixing in one skyline. Hold three numbers — **3,300 / 1,833 / 20** — three tower names — **Mingshi, Ruishi, Fangshi** — and one garden phrase — **Xiu Shen Li Ben** — and you can answer both scenic questions and interpretation passages on this site.

Thank you. I welcome your questions.
